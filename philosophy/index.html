Most AI products are built by technologists who looked at an industry from the outside and decided they understood it. I've spent my career doing the opposite — going inside first, earning fluency, then building from that position.

The Core Belief

Technology Is Easy. Domain Is Hard.

The tools to build AI products have never been more accessible. A capable engineer can assemble a working prototype in a weekend. This is not the hard part.

The hard part is knowing which problem is actually worth solving, understanding the workflow deeply enough to know where automation breaks down, and earning the trust of the domain expert who will decide whether your product lives or dies.

Most products fail not because the technology didn't work — but because the product was built for a version of the problem the builder imagined, not the problem the practitioner actually lives with every day.

"The best product decisions I've made came from time spent inside the workflow — not from reading industry reports or user surveys."
The Framework

Three Acts. Every Time.

Domain-first isn't a mindset — it's a repeatable process. The same three acts appear in every product I've built, whether at USPS or in my own companies.

ACT ONE

Go Inside

Before a spec, before a mockup, before a line of code — spend real time inside the domain. Shadow the practitioner. Do the work manually. Understand what they're afraid of, what they've tried before, and why it failed.

ACT TWO

Find the Real Problem

The stated problem is rarely the real problem. The real problem is usually one or two layers deeper — the constraint that makes the stated problem hard to solve, or the fear that makes the obvious solution unacceptable.

ACT THREE

Build the Fix

Now build — but build toward the real problem, not the surface one. This changes everything: what you build, how it's designed, how it's presented, and how you talk about it to the person who has to trust it with their work.

What This Looks Like in Practice

Domain-First vs. Tech-First

The difference between these two approaches shows up most clearly in regulated, high-stakes domains — where a wrong answer has real consequences and where the buyer is also the person whose career is on the line if it goes wrong.

TECH-FIRST APPROACH
Builds what's technically impressive, then looks for a user
Designs for the demo, not for the daily workflow
Treats domain expertise as a sales problem to overcome
Ships confidence — surfaces uncertainty as a bug
Measures success by features shipped
Learns about liability from a lawyer after the fact
DOMAIN-FIRST APPROACH
Starts with the practitioner's problem, then chooses the tool
Designs for the moment of highest stress in the workflow
Treats domain expertise as the product's most important input
Surfaces uncertainty explicitly — it's the trust mechanism
Measures success by decisions improved
Designs liability out of the system before writing the first spec

Why It Matters Now

AI Makes Domain Fluency More Important, Not Less

A common misconception: AI lowers the barrier to building in complex domains, so domain expertise matters less. The opposite is true.

AI makes it faster and cheaper to build a product that looks like it solves a domain problem. But it doesn't make it easier to know which problem to solve, or to earn the trust of the domain expert who will decide whether to use it.

In regulated industries — legal, health, logistics, finance, government — the decision to adopt an AI product is made by someone whose career, license, or liability is on the line. They're not evaluating your technology. They're evaluating whether you understand what you're asking them to trust.

Domain-first is how you answer that question before they ask it.
